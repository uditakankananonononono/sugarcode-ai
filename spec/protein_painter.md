# Protein Painter

Protein Painter: A de novo protein design tool that generates amino acid sequences from functional descriptions. It uses integrated structure prediction to verify folding and identifies active-site residues. Protein Painter predicts the stability of designed enzymes, allowing for the creation of custom biocatalysts for industrial applications and therapeutic interventions.

Protein Painter evolves from a sequence generator into a full-spectrum protein engineering environment that bridges molecular biology, structural biophysics, enzymology, and synthetic chemistry. At its core, it translates functional intent—such as “bind a tumor antigen,” “catalyze a redox reaction,” or “stabilize under acidic gut conditions”—into sequence designs by integrating protein language models with structural predictors like AlphaFold. But beyond sequence-to-structure mapping, it operates across the full central dogma: it evaluates transcriptional compatibility, codon usage, translation kinetics, and post-translational folding constraints to ensure that designed proteins are not just theoretically valid but biologically expressible in real host systems.
At the biochemical level, Protein Painter explicitly models enzyme active sites and binding interfaces using principles from physical chemistry and catalysis. It incorporates quantum chemistry-informed approximations (e.g., partial charge distributions, orbital overlap heuristics) to design catalytic residues capable of stabilizing transition states. The system predicts how amino acid substitutions influence reaction kinetics (kcat, Km) by simulating substrate positioning, hydrogen bonding networks, and electrostatic preorganization within the active site. For binding proteins, it evaluates ligand complementarity using docking-informed scoring—hydrophobic packing, π–π interactions, salt bridges—ensuring that the designed protein achieves both specificity and high affinity.
From a cellular biology perspective, Protein Painter accounts for the intracellular environment in which the protein will function. It predicts protein trafficking signals, secretion pathways, and compartment-specific folding constraints (e.g., disulfide bond formation in the ER vs. cytosol). It also models degradation pathways by identifying motifs that trigger proteasomal targeting or autophagy. For therapeutic proteins, it integrates immunogenicity prediction, scanning for epitopes that may trigger immune responses, and suggests sequence modifications to reduce antigenicity while preserving function. This ensures that designs are not only functional but also viable within living systems.
On the synthetic biology side, Protein Painter interfaces directly with genetic construct design. It generates DNA sequences optimized for specific hosts (E. coli, yeast, mammalian cells), balancing GC content, codon adaptation index (CAI), and mRNA secondary structure to maximize translation efficiency. It can design fusion proteins, add purification tags, or integrate regulatory elements such as inducible promoters and ribosome binding sites. Additionally, it simulates expression burden on the host cell, predicting metabolic costs and suggesting design modifications to avoid toxicity or growth inhibition—bridging protein engineering with systems biology constraints.
Finally, Protein Painter closes the loop with experimental and evolutionary feedback. It can simulate mutational scanning experiments, predicting how single or combinatorial mutations affect stability and function, and guiding directed evolution strategies. Integration with molecular dynamics and docking modules allows it to refine designs iteratively based on conformational flexibility and ligand interactions. The output is not just a sequence, but a complete experimental blueprint: annotated protein structures, predicted biochemical properties, expression constructs, and validation strategies. In this way, Protein Painter becomes a true end-to-end protein engineering platform—merging AI, chemistry, and biology into a unified system capable of designing functional biomolecules from first principles.
