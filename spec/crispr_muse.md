# CRISPR Muse

CRISPR Muse: A reinforcement-learning-based gRNA generator that refines its designs through simulated lab feedback loops. It predicts compatibility with various PAM sites and adjusts its models to improve accuracy. CRISPR Muse enables an iterative design process, ensuring that genetic interventions are continuously optimized for the specific requirements of the biosystem.

CRISPR Muse evolves beyond a conventional reinforcement-learning gRNA generator into a closed-loop, self-optimizing design system that integrates probabilistic genome editing models with experimentally grounded feedback signals. At its core, Muse operates on a policy-gradient RL framework where the action space consists of nucleotide-level edits to candidate gRNA sequences, and the reward function is dynamically shaped by multi-objective optimization across predicted on-target activity (via deep CNN/transformer hybrid scoring), off-target risk (modeled through whole-genome alignment embeddings), chromatin accessibility (ATAC-seq priors), and local DNA repair outcomes. Unlike static scoring tools, Muse treats guide design as a sequential decision-making process, iteratively refining guides by simulating editing outcomes across heterogeneous genomic contexts and learning from both successful and failed edits encoded as state transitions.
The system integrates a differentiable surrogate of cellular DNA repair pathways, enabling it to anticipate not only cleavage efficiency but also downstream repair distributions (HDR vs NHEJ vs MMEJ) conditioned on sequence context, microhomology patterns, and epigenetic state. This is achieved through training on large-scale CRISPR screening datasets and high-resolution repair outcome maps, allowing Muse to generate guides that are not only efficient cutters but are biased toward desired editing outcomes (e.g., precise indels, template incorporation). For prime editing and base editing, Muse extends its architecture to co-design pegRNAs and editing windows by modeling reverse transcriptase processivity, flap resolution kinetics, and local sequence thermodynamics.
CRISPR Muse also incorporates a simulated experimental feedback loop through integration with in silico “digital lab” environments, where predicted editing outcomes are iteratively validated against synthetic datasets generated from stochastic genome editing simulators. These simulators model cell-to-cell variability, delivery efficiency distributions, and clonal expansion dynamics, allowing Muse to refine its policies under realistic biological noise. Over time, the system builds a continuously updated posterior over guide performance, effectively functioning as a Bayesian optimizer that converges on globally optimal designs rather than local heuristics.
A key differentiator is Muse’s ability to generalize across CRISPR systems and PAM compatibilities. It embeds Cas variants (SpCas9, SaCas9, Cas12a, CasX, engineered PAM-flexible nucleases) into a shared latent space, enabling cross-nuclease transfer learning. This allows the model to recommend not just a guide, but an entire editing configuration—selecting the optimal nuclease, PAM context, spacer sequence, and delivery constraints for a given genomic locus. It further predicts compatibility with multiplexed editing scenarios, minimizing guide–guide interference and maximizing combinatorial editing efficiency in gene circuit engineering or large-scale perturbation screens.
Finally, CRISPR Muse is designed as a continuously learning system that ingests real-world experimental outputs through API integrations with sequencing pipelines (amplicon NGS, GUIDE-seq, CIRCLE-seq). These empirical results are used to fine-tune its internal models via reinforcement learning with human-in-the-loop corrections, enabling adaptive improvement over time. In its fully realized form, Muse becomes not just a design tool but an autonomous genome engineering strategist—capable of proposing, testing, and refining CRISPR interventions with a level of precision and adaptability that approaches experimental intuition.
CRISPR Muse transforms from a static guide RNA generator into a reinforcement learning–driven adaptive design system that continuously refines genome editing strategies through simulated experimental feedback and probabilistic modeling of cellular repair outcomes. At its core, the platform is built on a policy optimization framework (e.g., proximal policy optimization or deep Q-learning) where candidate gRNA sequences are treated as actions within an environment defined by genomic context, nuclease constraints, and cellular repair pathways. The reward function is multi-objective, incorporating predicted on-target cleavage efficiency, off-target penalty landscapes, editing precision (especially for base and prime editors), and downstream functional impact, enabling CRISPR Muse to learn optimal sequence design strategies that go beyond heuristic or supervised learning approaches.
Unlike conventional tools that rely solely on pre-trained datasets, CRISPR Muse simulates iterative “in silico experiments” by integrating predictive models of DNA repair pathways such as non-homologous end joining (NHEJ), microhomology-mediated end joining (MMEJ), and homology-directed repair (HDR). It generates probabilistic repair outcome distributions for each candidate edit, including indel spectra, frameshift likelihoods, and precise edit incorporation rates. For prime editing, the system models flap equilibration dynamics, mismatch repair bias, and reverse transcriptase extension efficiency, feeding these outcomes back into the reinforcement loop. This allows the model to optimize not just for cleavage, but for desired genomic outcomes, which is a fundamentally harder and more realistic objective.
CRISPR Muse also incorporates PAM flexibility and nuclease engineering into its decision space, allowing it to dynamically select between Cas variants (e.g., SpCas9-NGG, xCas9-NG, Cas12a-TTTN) based on local sequence constraints. It evaluates spacer–PAM compatibility jointly with chromatin accessibility and sequence context, enabling it to discover non-obvious editing strategies in regions previously considered “undruggable” due to PAM limitations. Additionally, it integrates RNA secondary structure prediction and gRNA folding stability into its optimization loop, ensuring that designed guides maintain structural integrity and effective Cas binding in vivo.
A critical innovation is the incorporation of simulated lab feedback loops grounded in real experimental noise models. CRISPR Muse injects stochastic variability into editing outcomes—such as transfection efficiency, Cas9 expression variability, and sequencing error profiles—to train models that are robust to real-world experimental conditions. Over time, the system can be fine-tuned with actual user-generated data, enabling a form of continual learning where the model adapts to specific lab conditions, cell types, or delivery methods. This effectively turns CRISPR Muse into a lab-specific intelligence layer that improves with usage, rather than a static prediction tool.
At the interface and systems level, CRISPR Muse outputs ranked gRNA strategies with full causal traceability, including why certain sequences were favored based on reward decomposition (e.g., reduced off-target risk vs increased repair precision). It can generate multi-step editing plans, such as sequential edits or combinatorial targeting strategies, and export them into executable experimental protocols or simulation-ready formats. By combining reinforcement learning, repair pathway modeling, and adaptive feedback integration, CRISPR Muse represents a shift toward self-optimizing genome engineering systems, where design is not just predicted, but continuously improved through interaction with biological complexity.
